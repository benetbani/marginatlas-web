The honest take

London rewards the operator who treats rent as the first line of the plan, not an afterthought.

The headline is a deep, wealthy market. The catch is that the same depth that fills your room also bids up the rent on it, so the businesses that last here are the ones priced and sited for that from day one.

  • Spending power is high and broad, so a strong offer finds its customers.
  • Holding space is the single heaviest cost, and the best sites rarely reach the open market.
  • Visitors thicken the trade in the centre, but the steady money is the residents and the office workers.
The local customer

Who you are selling to, and how freely they spend

Pay is high, but it spreads wide: a large lower-paid service workforce sits under a thick band of high earners in finance, tech, and the professions. A neighbourhood can swing the spending base more than the city average suggests.

What space costs

Space is the expensive part, and the gap between a prime pitch and a side street is enormous.

A high-street unit in the centre commands some of the steepest rents anywhere, and the best sites change hands quietly before they reach an agent. A street over, or a floor up, the numbers change completely. Negotiate the rent-free fit-out period; the headline rent rarely moves.

Tourist vs local

Visitors thicken the centre, but residents and office workers are the steady money.

Sixteen million arrivals a year fill the central pitches and lift the summer and December trade. Step out of the tourist core and the customer is local: people who live and work here, who come back through the slow months a visitor never sees. Build for the residents; let the visitors be the upside.

Best areas

Where to set up, by what you are opening

No single area suits every business. These are the parts of London that fit a given trade.

  • West EndRestaurants, bars, flagship retail

    The deepest footfall in the country, residents, office workers, and visitors together. Rents match it.

  • City and Canary WharfLunch trade, coffee, quick service

    A weekday office crowd with money and no time. Dead at the weekend, so build for five days, not seven.

  • East LondonIndependent food, design studios, third-wave coffee

    The fastest-growing independent trade, a younger crowd, and rents still below the centre, though climbing.

  • Residential high streetsSalons, clinics, childcare, neighbourhood cafes

    Steady local demand that does not depend on visitors, and the footfall a hybrid-working week now favours.

How it is changing

The centre of gravity keeps drifting east, and the high street keeps reinventing what it sells.

Two real directions shape where a new operator should look. The eastern districts that were warehouses a generation ago now carry the city's fastest-growing food, design, and tech trade, which is where rents have climbed hardest and footfall has followed. At the same time the traditional retail high street has thinned, and the units that survive lean on experience, food, and services that a screen cannot replace.

  • The eastern districts carry the fastest-growing independent trade, and the steepest rent rises with it.
  • Pure retail keeps giving ground to food, services, and experience on the high street.
  • Hybrid working has shifted weekday footfall out of the financial core and toward the residential neighbourhoods.
