# Section analysis: Home (the marketing front door)

## Who is on this page and what they came for

This is the front door, so the traffic is mixed, but it skews to the two cold-arrival personas. **P5 (the curious / SEO arrival)** lands here from a search or a share and is asking, half-skeptically, "does this thing actually have a real number for the business and place I care about, or is it another estimate-generator?" **P1 (the prospective owner)** arrives with a live decision and wants to know in five seconds whether this tool will help them make it. **P3/P4 (investor, lender, consultant, broker)** show up via word of mouth or a pricing search and are scanning for one thing: is this credible enough to cite, and is there depth behind the front page. The single question this page must answer for all of them: **"Is there a real, specific, holdable number waiting for *my* business in *my* place, and can I trust it?"** Everything below the hero is either proof of "real" or proof of "trust," and any section that proves neither is dead weight.

## Section-by-section audit

### 0a. Global chrome: full navbar
- **Job (T2):** Orient and let any persona jump to Countries / Industries / Cities / Compare; carry the "Get the data" CTA. Serves all personas as plumbing.
- **Useful or slop (T1):** Useful, non-negotiable. But the current nav is thin for an "atlas." Four flat top-level links with no preview of the catalog's scale undersell the product's biggest asset, which is breadth. The "Get the data" CTA is ambiguous (download? signup? pricing?), and on a tool whose whole pitch is "free to read," a hard commercial CTA at top-right slightly fights the "every benchmark is free" message one band down.
- **Cringe check (T3):** Low cringe risk. The one fix: "Get the data" reads like a generic SaaS button. Make it a search affordance or "Browse the atlas" so the very first click points at the product, not a paywall. The wordmark "Margin **Atlas**" is good and brand-true.
- **Best visual (T4):** A sticky blurred bar is correct. The upgrade is a **mega-menu on the topic dropdowns** (e.g. hovering Countries shows a few flagship countries + "all 60+"), which is the highest-ROI place on the whole page to communicate breadth, and a navbar mega-menu is the standard, non-cringe way to do it.
- **Redundancy/Depth/Honesty:** No redundancy. Depth: add a coverage hint in the menu. Honesty: a menu count must be real ("60+ countries" only if true), no vanity inflation.
- **VERDICT: REFORM.** Rename the CTA to a product-first action; turn the topic links into mega-menus that preview catalog breadth.

### 1. Hero: rotating question + search
- **Job (T2):** State the one claim ("we tell you what a business makes in a place") and hand the visitor the search box as the answer-path. Serves P1, P5 primarily; reassures all.
- **Useful or slop (T1):** Strongly useful, and the single best decision on the page: the rotating Newsreader question *is* the value prop, and putting the search directly under it makes the CTA the product itself. The deliberate choice to carry **no number** in the hero is right (no canonical figure to crown, keeps the focal point singular).
- **Cringe check (T3):** Two real risks. (1) The subtitle "Know if a business works before you risk your money" speaks only to P1; it ignores P3/P4 and the curious P5. (2) The navigator is a *frozen, inert* mock with two pre-filled fields and no visible affordance that these are editable, so a cold visitor may not realize it is the search. Sharpen by making the fields visibly interactive (caret, placeholder greys, a dropdown chevron) and by widening the subtitle to "Real numbers for a real trade in a real place. Free to read." Avoid the generic-startup cadence.
- **Best visual (T4):** The rotating-question-over-search pattern is the best possible hero for this product; no alternative beats it (a hero number would lie, a hero chart would over-claim, a hero illustration would dilute trust). The only debate is whether to add a faint live-search autocomplete preview; recommend keeping it clean, the rotation carries the motion.
- **Redundancy/Depth/Honesty:** No redundancy. Depth is intentionally low (correct). Honesty: clean, no number to mis-state.
- **VERDICT: KEEP** (with copy tweak to the subtitle and a clearer "this is editable" search affordance).

### 2. Live example tiles
- **Job (T2):** Deliver the first real numbers as instant proof that the search leads somewhere real; bait the curious into one click. Serves P5, P1.
- **Useful or slop (T1):** Very useful, this is the page's "show, don't tell." Three real take-home numbers ($31K Barcelona restaurants, $118K SF developers, $96K UK law firms) do more for credibility than any tagline. Risk: three hand-picked tiles can read as cherry-picked and the heading "Numbers people actually search for" is a claim the page can't see (it implies search-volume data).
- **Cringe check (T3):** The headline is slightly cringe (asserting popularity it can't show). Sharper: "Three real benchmarks, opened." Each tile says "Owner keeps about this a year" three times, which is repetitive; vary the framing (one take-home, one revenue, one margin) to teach that the tool reports *several* numbers, not just one. Surprising upgrade: pick three tiles that *contradict intuition* (a glamorous trade that keeps little; a boring trade that keeps a lot), which is far more shareable and on-brand for "honest read."
- **Best visual (T4):** Stat-cards are right. Debated alternative: a single "anatomy of one number" tile (one cell shown in miniature with its per-$100 split) would teach depth better, but loses the breadth signal. Best pick: **keep the 3-up stat tiles but make each a different number-type**, so the grid teaches both breadth and depth.
- **Redundancy/Depth/Honesty:** Mild overlap with the newsletter's sample-report preview (both show "what the owner keeps"). Depth: add a one-word "why" hook per tile (e.g. "rent" / "salaries"). Honesty: numbers are real and sanity-floored, good; drop the unprovable "people actually search for."
- **VERDICT: REFORM.** Re-headline, vary the three number-types, choose counterintuitive examples.

### 3. World map city picker
- **Job (T2):** Prove geographic breadth and offer an exploratory, low-commitment entry by place. Serves P5 (delight), P1/P4 (coverage check).
- **Useful or slop (T1):** Borderline. As the brand-signature surface it earns its place on delight and breadth-signaling, but **four pins is a coverage liability, not a coverage flex.** A product claiming an atlas that shows only San Francisco, Barcelona, Lagos, Tokyo invites the exact doubt the page is trying to kill ("so they only have four cities?"). Right now the map entertains (T1 pass) but under-proves (T6 fail).
- **Cringe check (T3):** The risk is "decorative world map," the single most generic SaaS hero motif. It is saved here by being a *navigator* not a backdrop, but only if pins feel dense and alive. Make it sharp by **showing real coverage density** (dozens of muted dots, four highlighted), so the eye reads "this is everywhere" not "this is four places."
- **Best visual (T4):** The stylized clickable map is the right brand call; do not turn it into a choropleth or bubble-by-value (would imply cross-geography ranking, an honesty violation). Debated alternative: a searchable city list beside the map for the keyboard-first P4. Best pick: **keep the bespoke map, add many muted coverage dots + a plain real count ("benchmarks in 60+ countries"), and pair it with a compact "or browse by country" list** for non-mouse users.
- **Redundancy/Depth/Honesty:** No redundancy (it is the only place-based entry). Depth is currently thin (4 pins). Honesty: the no-vanity-counter rule is good, but a *real* coverage number is honest and badly needed here; the constraint should be "real count, not inflated," not "no count."
- **VERDICT: REFORM.** Add real coverage density + a real (not vanity) count + a list fallback. The map stays; its emptiness is the problem.

### 4. State comparison
- **Job (T2):** Show the comparison superpower and demonstrate the honesty discipline (like-for-like, no winner). Serves P2/P3/P4 (benchmarking) and teaches all personas the brand's intellectual honesty.
- **Useful or slop (T1):** Useful as a *capability demo*, but weak as content on the front door. "Restaurants across four US states, typical revenue" with neutral non-ranked bars is, by design, a comparison that refuses to tell you anything actionable ("read each on its own terms, no league table"). That is honest and on-brand, but a first-time visitor sees four similar bars and a caveat that says "this doesn't mean what you think," which is anticlimactic. It proves rigor but not value.
- **Cringe check (T3):** The non-cringe risk here is the opposite of usual: it is *so* careful it reads as withholding. Make it land by comparing something with a **genuinely surprising spread** and by stating the structural reason ("the gap is rent and wages, not demand"), so the honesty caveat protects a real insight rather than neutralizing a flat one. Also, revenue is the wrong metric to lead with on a margin product, show take-home, the thing the brand is about.
- **Best visual (T4):** Like-for-like horizontal bars with no crown are the right primitive and the honesty rail is load-bearing (keep the kit, not a generic Recharts bar that would auto-sort and crown a leader). Debated alternative: a small-multiples "same trade, three places, per-$100 split" would prove the margin story better than revenue bars. Recommend **switching the metric to take-home / margin** and keeping the bar grammar.
- **Redundancy/Depth/Honesty:** Some thematic overlap with the map (both are "we cover many places"). Depth: add the structural "why the gap" line. Honesty: model behavior, keep exactly.
- **VERDICT: REFORM.** Keep the honest bar primitive; change the metric to take-home and add the one-line structural reason so it proves an insight, not just rigor.

### 5. Neighbourhood proof cards
- **Job (T2):** Show granularity goes below the city ("texture behind the numbers") and add human warmth. Serves P5 (delight), P1 (this place feels real).
- **Useful or slop (T1):** Closest section to slop on the page. The cards (Queens, Le Marais, Shitamachi) are charming travel-guide copy ("the best Chinese food," "the oldest planned square") but carry **no numbers and no economic content**, on a page whose entire promise is numbers. It rewards attention (T1 entertain) but changes no decision (T1 useful fail) and risks reading as filler that wandered in from a tourism site.
- **Cringe check (T3):** High cringe risk: this is the section most likely to read as "AI wrote travel blurbs." It is also where "no fabricated place detail" is hardest to hold. Sharpen by tying each neighbourhood detail to an *economic* fact the atlas actually has ("Le Marais: boutique rents that halve a clothing store's take-home vs the Paris average"), turning tourism trivia into margin texture, which is the brand.
- **Best visual (T4):** Gallery cards are fine, but the content is the problem, not the visual. If reformed to carry a neighbourhood-level number, a compact card with a tier chip + one stat beats the current pure-prose card.
- **Redundancy/Depth/Honesty:** Overlaps the map (both prove geographic granularity, neither with economics). Depth: currently decorative. Honesty: the no-fabrication rule makes this section fragile and it self-omits, a sign it is structurally weak.
- **VERDICT: REFORM or CUT.** If it can carry a real neighbourhood-level economic fact, reform it into "granularity proof." If it can only carry travel prose, **cut it**, it dilutes a numbers product.

### 6. Audience band
- **Job (T2):** Tell each persona "this is for you" and (critically) signal the monetizable depth audience (P3/P4) without making them the subject. Serves all, especially P3/P4.
- **Useful or slop (T1):** Useful and well-judged. The four roles (PE/investors, agencies, consultants, founders) correctly frame the high-value personas as *clients*, and the use-lines are crisp and decision-relevant ("sanity-check a target before the first call"). This is the page's clearest expression of "who pays and why."
- **Cringe check (T3):** Medium risk, "who it's for" icon grids are a SaaS cliché. It survives because the lines are specific. Keep them specific; never let them drift to "for teams of all sizes." One sharpening: order the four to put the *buyer* personas first deliberately (already the case) and consider replacing generic line-icons with a small concrete detail per role.
- **Best visual (T4):** 4-up icon feature grid is appropriate and intentionally low-density (the "breathing" beat). Alternative considered: a single sentence per audience in a quiet list. The grid is better for scannability. Keep.
- **Redundancy/Depth/Honesty:** No redundancy. Depth is appropriately light. Honesty: clients-not-subjects framing is correct and must be preserved.
- **VERDICT: KEEP** (tighten icons; hold the specificity).

### 7. Pricing teaser
- **Job (T2):** Establish "free to read" up front and preview paid depth without a checkout. Serves P1/P5 (free reassurance), P3/P4 (what depth costs).
- **Useful or slop (T1):** Useful and necessary on a freemium tool. Leading with "Every benchmark is free to read" is the right trust move. The three-tier card grammar is clear and prices come from the shared `TIERS` constant (cannot drift), good.
- **Cringe check (T3):** Low-to-medium. Full pricing tables on a homepage can feel like a premature ask. Mitigated here by it being a teaser with no checkout. Risk: it sits among four other card grids and blends in. Sharpen by emphasizing the *free* tier visually (the hero of this section is "$0, read anything"), not the Premium card.
- **Best visual (T4):** Three tier cards are conventional and fine. Debated alternative: a single "free vs paid" two-line strip ("Read every benchmark free. Paid adds quartiles, comparison, and export.") would be quieter and less card-wall, deferring the full table to /pricing. Recommend **demoting to a slimmer free-vs-paid strip** to reduce the card-grid monotony and keep the homepage from feeling like a checkout.
- **Redundancy/Depth/Honesty:** No redundancy (only pricing surface). Honesty: real prices, good.
- **VERDICT: REFORM** (slim from a 3-card table to a free-led teaser strip; full tiers live on /pricing).

### 8. Blog rail
- **Job (T2):** Prove there is a thinking human behind the data (editorial authority) and feed SEO/returning visitors. Serves P3/P4 (credibility), P5 (SEO surface).
- **Useful or slop (T1):** Moderately useful. The three titles are genuinely good and on-brand ("Why bakery margins in Tokyo are half what they are in Paris" is exactly the brand's voice). It signals the team understands *why* numbers differ, which builds trust with sophisticated personas. But it is low on the conversion path and adds a fourth card grid.
- **Cringe check (T3):** Low risk if posts are real. The token-gradient covers with a single initial letter are a tasteful fallback, not cringe. Keep titles opinionated and specific, never "5 tips for small business."
- **Best visual (T4):** 3-up blog cards are standard and correct. Alternative: a single featured post + two text links would be quieter. The 3-up is fine if a non-card section sits adjacent (rhythm). Keep.
- **Redundancy/Depth/Honesty:** No redundancy. Honesty: real posts only; fallback covers are fine.
- **VERDICT: KEEP** (ensure real posts; consider moving lower so card grids don't stack).

### 9. Newsletter / free report
- **Job (T2):** Capture email via a genuine value exchange (the free benchmark report) and preview the payload (the per-$100 split). Serves P1/P4 (lead magnet), all (final soft CTA).
- **Useful or slop (T1):** Useful and well-built. The two-column panel (copy + form left, **real** miniature per-$100 report right) is the page's strongest "show the payload" moment, and the sample bar (COGS $30 / payroll $33 / rent $18 / else $12 / owner keeps $7, summing to 100, moss on the kept slice) is the clearest single illustration of the brand's core grammar on the whole page.
- **Cringe check (T3):** Low. The "no placeholder image slot" rule is honored with a real render, which is exactly right. The bullets are plain and good. One nit: "you@company.com" placeholder skews B2B; the core P1/P5 are often not "company" people. Use "you@email.com."
- **Best visual (T4):** The real sample-report preview is the best possible visual here, far better than a generic newsletter banner; it doubles as proof of the product. Keep it. In fact, this per-$100 grammar is so strong it is arguably under-used, see "what's missing."
- **Redundancy/Depth/Honesty:** Mild overlap with the example tiles (both show "what the owner keeps"). Honesty: explicitly tagged "Sample preview" and "not a live read," exemplary.
- **VERDICT: KEEP** (minor placeholder copy tweak).

### 0b. Global chrome: rich footer
- **Job (T2):** Sitewide navigation, SEO link equity, legal/trust strip. Plumbing for all.
- **Useful or slop (T1):** Useful, standard. The blurb restates the value prop well. Columns (Explore / Product / Company) are sensible.
- **Cringe check (T3):** None. Keep the honest legal strip (it correctly flags the mockup numbers as illustrative).
- **Best visual (T4):** Multi-column dark footer is correct.
- **Redundancy/Depth/Honesty:** Honest legal strip is a credibility asset; keep it on the live page too (real disclosure, not mockup-only).
- **VERDICT: KEEP.**

### X. Failure-state variant (review-only)

## What is MISSING, new sections to ADD

The page proves "we have numbers" well, but barely proves **"you can trust the numbers"** and **"the numbers are deep,"** which is exactly what converts the skeptical P5 and the paying P3/P4. The biggest gaps:

1. **"How we get to the number" / trust strip.** The whole product is "honest numbers, not estimates," yet the page never shows *how* a number is built. A compact, calm strip, "We start from real filings and surveys, blend like-for-like, and show you the spread, not a single guess", with a one-line methodology link. **Job:** convert skepticism into trust. **Persona:** P3/P4 (citation-grade), P5 (reassurance). **Visual:** a 3-step horizontal "raw data → like-for-like blend → the spread you see" micro-diagram (not icons-with-platitudes). This is the single most important missing section.

2. **"Anatomy of one benchmark" / what you actually get.** A reader cannot tell from the homepage what a cell page contains. One expanded example showing median + the decile spread + the per-$100 split + the honest read, in miniature. **Job:** show the depth that justifies paying. **Persona:** P1, P3/P4. **Visual:** a single annotated cell-card miniature (reuse the per-$100 bar + a small decile range). This proves T6 depth the current page lacks.

3. **The "honest read" voice sample.** The brand promises "the number, the reason, and an honest read," but the homepage shows numbers and reasons, never the *honest read* (the editorial judgment that is the actual differentiator). A single pulled quote-block: a real one-paragraph honest read for one cell ("Coffee shops here look profitable on revenue, but rent and a six-month ramp mean most owners take home less than a salaried barista for two years"). **Job:** demonstrate the irreplaceable human judgment. **Persona:** P1, P5 (shareable), P3/P4 (this is what they cite). **Visual:** a Newsreader pull-quote block on warm ground, the page's second editorial moment.

4. **A coverage/scale proof line (real, not vanity).** Tied to the map fix: one honest stat line ("X trades across Y cities in Z countries, every one free to read"). **Job:** answer "is my thing in here?" at a glance. **Persona:** all. **Visual:** a single tabular-figure stat row under the map.

5. **(Optional, P1-specific) "Can I survive the slow start?" hook.** The P1 persona's deepest fear is the ramp, not the steady-state number. A small teaser ("See the first-24-months cash curve, not just the typical year") would speak directly to the core persona's real question. **Job:** address the survival fear. **Persona:** P1. **Visual:** a tiny ramp sparkline. Add only if the data exists; otherwise hold.

## What to CUT or MERGE

- **CUT (or hard-reform) Neighbourhood proof cards (5).** It is the weakest section: travel-guide prose with no economics on a numbers product, the highest cringe/fabrication risk, and it self-omits (the architecture itself signals it is fragile). If it cannot carry a real neighbourhood-level *number*, remove it; its "granularity" job is partly covered by the map and far better served by the new "anatomy of one benchmark" section.
- **MERGE State comparison (4) + the map (3)'s coverage message into one "breadth + comparison" beat is not advised** (they are different jobs), but **merge the redundant "owner keeps" framing** that appears in tiles (2), state comparison (if kept as revenue), and the newsletter preview (9): pick *one* canonical "what the owner keeps" visual (the per-$100 split) and let the others vary their metric so the page teaches more than one number.
- **SLIM Pricing teaser (7)** from a full 3-card table to a free-led strip; the full table belongs on /pricing and currently adds to the card-wall.
- **Watch the card-grid monotony:** after reforms, the page still risks tiles → cards → cards → cards → cards. The map (3) and newsletter panel (9) are the only two circuit-breakers. Cutting neighbourhood cards (5) and slimming pricing (7) directly fixes this, which is a structural argument for the cuts above, not just a content one.

## The reformed, re-ordered section list (final recommendation)

1. **Navbar (mega-menu)** , sitewide nav + product-first CTA; mega-menus preview catalog breadth. *Visual: sticky blurred bar + topic mega-menus.*
2. **Hero: rotating question + search** , the one claim + the search as the answer-path; widened subtitle. *Visual: Newsreader rotating question over an editable navigator.*
3. **Live example tiles (reformed)** , first real numbers as proof; three *different* number-types, counterintuitive picks, re-headlined. *Visual: 3-up stat cards.*
4. **Anatomy of one benchmark (NEW)** , show the depth a cell actually contains (median + spread + per-$100 + honest-read snippet). *Visual: one annotated cell-card miniature.* (Circuit-breaker: not a plain card grid.)
5. **World map city picker (reformed)** , place-based entry + real coverage density and an honest count + list fallback. *Visual: bespoke stylized map, many muted dots, few accent pins, real stat line.*
6. **How we get to the number (NEW trust strip)** , convert skepticism to trust. *Visual: 3-step raw→blend→spread micro-diagram.*
7. **State comparison (reformed)** , the honest like-for-like superpower, now on take-home with the structural "why the gap" line. *Visual: kit like-for-like bars, no crown.*
8. **The honest read (NEW pull-quote)** , demonstrate the irreplaceable editorial judgment. *Visual: Newsreader pull-quote on warm ground.* (Second editorial circuit-breaker.)
9. **Audience band** , who it's for, buyers framed as clients. *Visual: 4-up icon feature grid.*
10. **Newsletter / free report** , value-exchange email capture with the real per-$100 payload preview. *Visual: two-column panel + real sample-report mini.*
11. **Blog rail** , editorial authority + SEO; moved low so card grids don't stack. *Visual: 3-up blog cards.*
12. **Pricing teaser (slimmed)** , "free to read, paid adds depth," as a strip not a table. *Visual: free-led teaser strip, full tiers deferred to /pricing.*
13. **Footer** , sitewide nav, SEO equity, honest legal/disclosure strip. *Visual: multi-column dark footer.*

Rationale for the new order: it front-loads *proof of real* (tiles, anatomy), then *proof of breadth* (map), then *proof of trust* (how-we-get-the-number, honest comparison, the honest read), then the soft asks (audience, newsletter, blog, pricing). Neighbourhood cards are cut; the two new editorial/trust sections (6, 8) and the new depth section (4) replace the page's biggest gap, demonstrating that the numbers are deep and trustworthy, not just present, while doubling as the rhythm circuit-breakers that keep the page from reading as a wall of identical cards.
